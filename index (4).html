It's Only
Some Animal

How Kazakhstan punishes animal cruelty on paper and loses track of it in practice — and the AI registry that could change that.

By Zhampeissova Makhabbat Scroll ↓

On 22 September 2026, a 43-year-old man in Zhambyl region bit a cat on a live TikTok stream. Hundreds watched. Police identified him, issued a fine, and reported: "Домашнее животное не пострадало" — the pet was not harmed.[1]

01 — The Problem

A law that exists, and doesn't.

Kazakhstan has the laws. Article 316 of the Criminal Code criminalises cruelty to animals. The 2021 Law "On Responsible Treatment of Animals" declares animals "capable of experiencing pain and suffering."[2] Law No. 292-VIII, signed 19 May 2026, raised maximum criminal sanctions to three years.[3]

What Kazakhstan does not have is a system that makes these laws work. Over 10.5 years, 417 cases were filed under Article 316. Only 31 reached trial.[4] That is a prosecution rate of 7.4 per cent.

Even when they reach court, sentences are light. In 2020, a man in Aktau was caught on CCTV jumping on a cat's head. His sentence: 60 hours of community work. The court defended the sentence on Facebook after public outcry.[5] In Kentau, a man beat his neighbour's dog, Sky, with a shovel. Sky survived with a hip fracture, spinal cord contusion and concussion. The case was dismissed because "no injuries were found on the dog."[6]

The social-science question: why does a state with laws against cruelty fail to enforce them, and what would make enforcement visible? This is not a question of missing legislation. It is a question of missing infrastructure.

02 — Background & Data

The numbers Kazakhstan keeps, and the ones it doesn't.

There are two kinds of killing to count. Cruelty by individuals, which Article 316 is meant to punish. And culling by the state, which Law No. 292-VIII explicitly legalised in July 2026. The first is undercounted. The second is counted in two incompatible ways, and in neither is the question asked whether it works.

What the state does count

The General Prosecutor's Office recorded 127 criminal cruelty cases in 2025, up 18 per cent year on year.[14] The Ministry of Interior recorded 74,296 Article 73 cases in 2024 and 82,478 in 2025 — of which 55 per cent were dismissed with no action.[15]

The state also counts what it kills. Per the World Organisation for Animal Health, in the first 8 months of 2023 Kazakhstan caught 131,807 dogs and 7,421 cats; it killed 79 per cent of the dogs and 69 per cent of the cats.[16] In 2024, 85.3 per cent of 268,000 captured animals were killed. In 2025, of roughly 276,000 dogs captured, 12.6 per cent were sterilised.[17] Shymkent alone budgets 41 million tenge per year for culling.[18]

What the state does not count

At a WOAH regional seminar in December 2025, Kazakhstan's Ministry of Agriculture confirmed what activists have said for years: there is no unified database for dogs and cats.[16] The existing pet-registration platform, Tanba, is underused. Chipping is not mandatory. Each akimat sets its own capture and euthanasia timeframes under the May 2026 law, and the results are not aggregated.

03 — Research

What we know, by approach.

Three bodies of research sit around this problem. None has shaped Kazakhstan's enforcement design.

Approach 1

Kazakhstani legal scholarship

Lawyer Zhangeldy Suleymanov argues Article 316 is written so narrowly that police dismiss cases whenever the animal survives, as in Kentau.[6] The problem is a legal definition, and more fundamentally an enforcement one.

Approach 2

"The Link"

Addington & Randour (2024), using FBI NIBRS data, show that intentional animal cruelty overwhelmingly co-occurs with other crimes, often intimate partner and family violence.[21] Not used in Kazakhstan's domestic-violence policy.

Approach 3

US registry experience

Suffolk County, NY (2010) launched the first US animal-abuser registry; others followed. Shelters and employers screen applicants against it. The ASPCA and Harvard Law Review warn of carceral overreach — critiques this project engages but does not accept for Kazakhstan.[25][26][28]

The gap

Kazakhstani law diagnoses Article 316's weakness without proposing data infrastructure. "The Link" shows cruelty predicts human violence but hasn't reached Kazakhstan's screening architecture (childcare, teaching, animal work). The US registry model offers a tested institutional tool alongside documented failure modes that any Kazakhstani version must design around. This project operates at the intersection: the registry civil society has demanded, built at Kazakhstan's scale, designed around US failure modes.

04 — The AI-Assisted Solution

Tanba+: a registry for a visible system.

Extend Kazakhstan's existing pet-registration platform, Tanba, into a three-layer national registry. AI's role: pattern detection, standardised video processing, and the aggregation that makes dashboards possible. No decision is made by AI. Every flag triggers a human review.

Layer 01

Animal-side registry

Mandatory chipping. Every shelter intake logged. Every euthanasia requires a vet signature with licence number. Public dashboards per akimat, per month. Answers Demand 5 of the May 2026 e-Petition.

Layer 02

Case-outcome registry

Every Article 316 and Article 407 case logged with charge, verdict, sentence and judge name. Public and searchable. AI tags regional disparities the state won't publish on its own.

Layer 03

Public abuser registry

Anyone convicted under Article 316 (or second-offence under Articles 407-1/407-2) entered after final court conviction. Carries three concrete consequences.

What the public registry does

  1. Ownership ban: for the duration of sentence plus 3 years. Enforced through Layer 1's chipping system — registered abusers cannot legally register a new animal.
  2. Shelter adoption screening: every shelter must check applicants against the registry before placing an animal. A hit disqualifies.
  3. Employment screening for positions of trust: teachers, childcare workers, veterinary and shelter staff, animal-handling occupations (including capture-crew contractors). Sector-limited, not blanket — concentrates consequences where "the Link" research supports elevated risk.

Entries expire after 10 years from completion of sentence, provided no further conviction. Registered persons can challenge entry through a specified administrative-court process. Every entry requires final court conviction verified by the General Prosecutor's Office — AI never produces a registry entry.

Who runs it

Ministry of Ecology (the e-Petition's own addressee) as lead agency; Ministry of Internal Affairs for case-outcomes; General Prosecutor's Office verifies convictions before registry entry. Oversight commission with binding seats for Zverohelp Astana, Inucobo and StopOtlov Foundation.

Two real case studies

Suffolk County, NY animal-abuser registry (2010–).[25] First US jurisdiction to require convicted offenders to register for 10 years. Shelters consult it before adoption. Tanba+ closely tracks Suffolk's design — ten-year period, public access, shelter and employment screening — but differs on three points: Ministry of Ecology administers (not an SPCA); entries drawn directly from General Prosecutor's conviction records (not self-reported); AI-enabled at Kazakhstan's case volume (Suffolk is manual).

FBI NIBRS (US, 2014–). Adding animal cruelty to national reporting produced the "Link" research literature. Cautionary note: NIBRS is restricted-access, which has drawn methodological critique.[31] Kazakhstan's version should be public by default.

The Bill

Environmental

Modest compute: a database and dashboard, not a large model. But Kazakhstan's grid is ~70% coal. Image classification should be batched, not run per upload.

Economic

Funded from existing culling budgets (41 million KZT per city in Shymkent alone), not in addition. Cities face a real trade-off.

Labor

Veterinarians become mandatory reporters, with legal protection. Shelter workers' recording work rises short-term, drops as forms standardise.

Governance

Hosted on Kazakhstan servers per Law No. 230-VIII on AI (2025/26). The oversight commission is the only barrier between Layer 3 and surveillance abuse.

Data ethics

A public registry is, by design, punishment beyond the sentence. Entry requires final conviction only. Screening is sector-limited (children, dependent adults, animals). 10-year sunset. Challenge process in administrative court.

The three layers map directly to the seven demands of e-Petition П-2026-00000313 (8 May 2026). Layer 1 answers Demand 5 (mandatory unified database) and supports Demand 2 (standardised aggression assessment through video records, Demand 3). Layer 2 is the author's own addition: making court outcomes searchable responds to the 2020 Aktau court's need to defend itself on Facebook, where no one could find the actual ruling. Layer 3 synthesises the international "Link" literature with Kazakhstan's post-Saltanat Nukenova domestic-violence framework, but explicitly avoids the public-shaming model opposed by the ASPCA.

The oversight commission's design draws on comparative work: the Suffolk County registry's failures are read as the absence of civil-society binding seats, not merely the presence of a public list. Inucobo (led by Lillia Sarsenova), Zverohelp Astana, and StopOtlov Foundation have earned their claim to these seats through the 100,000-signature campaign against the May 2026 amendments; a registry built without them would re-centralise exactly the discretion the petition sought to constrain.

The Verdict

Yes, but only.

Should Kazakhstan build this? Yes — under five conditions. Without them, the registry becomes a more efficient killing machine, which is not what citizens asked for and not what the research supports.

  1. Public by default, downloadable by akimat and month. Dashboards that cannot be interrogated are decoration.
  2. Entries require final court conviction verified by the General Prosecutor's Office. 10-year sunset. Challengeable through administrative court. Never AI-generated.
  3. Sector-limited screening for positions of trust with children, dependent adults and animals. Not generalised to all employment.
  4. Funded from the existing culling budget, not added. Cities must face the choice.
  5. Civil-society seats on the oversight commission are legally binding, not advisory. Without binding seats, the public registry becomes a mechanism the state can abuse.

If these conditions hold, Tanba+ can do what punishment alone has not: make the state's own choices visible, give prosecutors evidence to raise the 7 per cent conviction rate, keep convicted abusers away from the shelters and schools where "the Link" research shows elevated risk, and connect cruelty records to the broader protection system Kazakhstan's 2024 Saltanat's Law already commits to. If they do not hold, this project's honest recommendation is that Kazakhstan should not build the registry at all: an unsupervised public register of names is a more dangerous institution than an under-enforced criminal code.

Punishment on paper is not accountability. Kazakhstan does not need a harsher law. It needs a record.
